*Breaking microbial dogma: outbreaks, ancient DNA and new species*

Professor Mark Pallen returns to discuss early microbial outbreak genomics and how unexpected results challenged established assumptions. With hosts Lee Katz, Andrew Page and Nabil-Fareed Alikhan, he traces his work from hospital outbreak genomics with Nick Loman to clinical metagenomics, ancient tuberculosis and the chicken gut microbiome. The conversation connects these discoveries with evidence checking, open science and the large proportion of microbial diversity still uncharacterised.

## In this episode

### From databases to outbreak genomics

Pallen recalls recruiting Nick Loman to Birmingham with five years of funding and the opportunity to complete a PhD. Their earlier database work included Colibase and CampyDB, but high-throughput sequencing opened a different direction: tracking multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii in hospital.

George Weinstock sequenced three isolates, allowing the team to identify SNPs and examine relationships between strains. Pallen describes this as the first Gram-negative genomic epidemiology paper, following earlier work on Staphylococcus aureus. A subsequent outbreak study expanded to over 70 genomes, as he recalls. The central question was whether genome differences could reveal how a hospital pathogen spread.

### The 2011 German outbreak and benchtop sequencers

Winning an Ion Torrent sequencer in a European competition gave Pallen and Loman an opportunity to learn its data analysis before the 2011 German Shiga toxin-producing E. coli outbreak. When a German group and BGI released outbreak sequence data, Loman analysed it and shared results through Twitter. Other bioinformaticians contributed resistance searches and comparisons with existing genomes.

The collaboration produced a New England Journal of Medicine paper after Pallen and Loman accepted a deadline of less than 48 hours to rewrite the paper and redo the genome analysis. A separate study compared Ion Torrent, 454 Junior and Illumina MiSeq using a British outbreak isolate. Pallen reports homopolymer-related accuracy problems with Ion Torrent and 454, while MiSeq performed substantially better. At recording, the comparison had received over 1,500 citations.

### Pathotypes, evidence trails and open science

The outbreak strain challenged tidy E. coli pathotype categories. It combined Shiga toxin production with enteroaggregative features but lacked the LEE-encoded type III secretion system. Pallen uses this to question assumptions that a familiar combination of virulence factors must always accompany disease.

The discussion extends to evidence trails: an often-repeated Darwin quotation may lack a genuine source, just as a protein annotation can propagate through sequence matches without adequate supporting evidence. The hosts also recall seeing detailed outbreak analyses appear on blogs and Twitter rather than waiting for journal publication. Pallen connects that experience with later open-science practices during COVID-19, including preprints and rapid sharing.

### Clinical metagenomics and pathogen blooms

After the outbreak collaboration, the team sequenced stored patient stool samples to test whether metagenomics could detect the outbreak organism. Pallen expected E. coli to be a minor component, perhaps yielding only a Shiga toxin gene. Instead, Loman reported examples with 20-fold and 50-fold coverage of the outbreak genome: infection had produced a substantial bacterial bloom.

Later intensive-care work found a related pattern. In some patients who had received multiple antibiotics, multidrug-resistant pathogens accounted for 80–90% of the microbial biomass, sometimes more than 90%. These findings challenged Pallen’s expectation that pathogens would increase without taking over the microbiome.

### Ancient tuberculosis and submerged sediments

Metagenomics of 200-year-old Hungarian mummy samples initially yielded seven-fold coverage of a tuberculosis genome. Further sampling recovered more than a dozen ancient genomes. Mixed bases, initially hidden by a pipeline’s filtering threshold, revealed closely related strains within individual samples. Most patients analysed had more than one strain, overturning Pallen’s assumption that tuberculosis infection involved only a single strain.

Another collaboration examined undersea sediments for evidence of the Neolithic transition. The intended targets were sheep and goat DNA, but the analysis instead detected wheat. The resulting Science paper reported wheat in the British Isles 8,000 years before present, around 2,000 years earlier than expected. Pallen describes this as evidence suggesting social connections between southern European Neolithic communities and northern Mesolithic peoples.

### Chicken gut genomes and the unexplored majority

Pallen recalls a 2012 chicken gut study that assembled genomes from shotgun metagenomic data using tetranucleotide-frequency binning, before the term MAGs became established. Later work revealed hundreds of new species: he recalls 800, distinguishing over 600 completely new species from others that had not been named. Giving these organisms names became another research direction.

In other animal-associated datasets, up to 80% of reads could remain unassigned by Kraken. Pallen argues that these sequences are not merely functionally unknown: their taxonomic origins are unknown too. He estimates that only 1–2% of microbial species have been genome-sequenced and characterised, and sees long reads and binning as routes to better genome recovery.

## Show notes

We are joined again by Prof Mark Pallen who takes us through his early experiences in high-
throughput microbial genomics. Mark was pleased that he persuaded Nick Loman to join him in
Birmingham. Mark tells us how they worked with George Weinstock to perform the first genome
sequence analyses of Gram-negatives for genomic epidemiology—in this case of multi-drug
resistant Acinetobacter baumannii.

After winning an Ion Torrent sequencer in a competition, Mark and Nick then contributed some
pioneering genomic analyses of the German STEC outbreak. One of their studies involved crowd
sourced approaches, primed by Twitter and was published in the New England Journal of Medicine;
the other provided a performance comparison of newly launched bench top sequencing platforms.
Mark, Lee and Nabil discuss how this outbreak overturned dogmas concerning the archetypal
status of pathotypes of E. coli. The conversation then moves on to the need for evidence trails
and challenging assumptions, whether annotating proteins or quoting Darwin (see
<https://colinpurrington.com/2012/02/darwin-on-the-floor-lhao/>). Nabil recalls the excitement
of realtime analysis of an epidemic and acknowledges the legacy of Mark and Nick's work in 2011
to current approaches to the Covid pandemic.

Mark describes his exciting experiences exploiting metagenomics in clinical and ancient DNA
contexts, including analysis of disease-associated stool samples and of 200-year TB genomes in
Hungarian mummies. Yet again this led to overturning of assumptions--in this case that people
only get infected with a single strain of M. tuberculosis. It turns out that multiple
infections were the norm 200 years ago.

Shortly afterwards, Pallen helped assemble a team that analysed undersea sediments shedding
light on the Neolithic transition in England and culminating in a Science paper. Mark then
takes us through his recent metagenomics analyses of critically ill patients and of the chicken
gut, emphasising the excitement of finding hundreds of new species in such a commonplace
setting. Mark finishes off by sharing his excitement that there is still so much of the
microbial world left for us to discover using sequencing and bioinformatics analyses. We are
just 2% of the way there!
