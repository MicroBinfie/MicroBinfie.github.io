*Contagion part 2: genome dashboards, phylogenies and the CDC*

Lee Katz, Andrew Page and Nabil-Fareed Alikhan continue their review of Contagion, examining its genome dashboard, phylogenetic trees and portrayal of CDC scientists. They compare the film’s laboratory scenes with bioinformatics work around 2011 and identify real CDC locations used during filming. Their discussion separates convincing scientific detail from dramatic shortcuts in disease progression, vaccine allocation and the international pandemic response.

## In this episode

### A convincing genome dashboard

The hosts begin with the genome dashboard shown around the film’s 28th or 29th minute. Its panels combine a sequence alignment, protein structure, recombination map and similarity comparisons with related sequences. They particularly like the way it brings these views together. They also clarify two shortcuts in the dialogue: bat and pig sequences mean sequences from viruses associated with those hosts, not the animals’ own genomes, and the molecular graphic depicts an attachment protein rather than an entire virus particle.

The interface reminds them of Jalview and Java desktop software, prompting a discussion of Swing. Nobody identifies the application, however, and they suspect it was made for the film. One host reports having read that the displayed sequence and protein came from Nipah virus, but explicitly cannot confirm this. Ian Lipkin is named as the scientific adviser; the hosts would like to know who designed the dashboard.

### What the phylogenetic tree does not establish

A later scene links a phylogenetic tree to a new R0. The hosts find this much less convincing than the dashboard. The scientist appears simply to open an image from an email attachment, in what looks like Microsoft Paint or the mail client’s viewer, rather than inspect a tree in specialist software. Its coloured branches and black background look more like a textbook illustration than their usual working output, although its long tip labels earn a point for realism.

They question the long branches, the absence of a scale bar and bootstrap values, and the lack of an obviously divergent lineage. A second view turns out to be a zoom into the blue Durban cluster, not a different tree. Their central objection is that the displayed tree does not justify the claimed change in R0. They contrast this shortcut with the film’s earlier explanation involving infectious period, contact rate and mode of transmission.

### Bioinformatics and sequencing around 2011

The film’s bioinformatician moves between computer work and the laboratory. Lee explains that CDC had very few dedicated bioinformaticians around 2011, making that combination more plausible then than the hosts would find it in an updated film. The discussion connects this changing division of labour with the spread of accessible sequencing instruments, particularly the MiSeq desktop sequencer.

Lee says they received their first MiSeq in 2012. He also recalls sequencing the major Haiti cholera genome using 36-base-pair reads on a GA2, while being unsure whether the instrument was a GA2X. The hosts discuss 454 and SOLiD alongside early Illumina sequencing, and remember Velvet as software built for very short reads. This leads to a terminology point: roughly 300-base-pair 454 reads once seemed long, while later expectations include 150-base paired-end reads and much longer sequences.

### Which CDC locations are real?

Lee remembers seeing preparations for Contagion while commuting to CDC, including staged protesters outside. He identifies the actual driveway, visitors’ car park and public museum area in the film. The other hosts recognise places they visited themselves. Lee also finds the fictional director’s route from the visitors’ car park through the museum amusing.

These recognisable exterior and public spaces do not make all the CDC scenes authentic: Lee says the interior conference-room scenes were not filmed at CDC. The hosts recommend the museum as a place visitors can explore. They contrast Contagion’s use of real CDC locations with The Walking Dead, which used the Cobb Energy Center as its stand-in.

### Disease progression and missing recoveries

In their closing assessment, the hosts question the film’s compressed disease timeline. One host describes an apparent progression from infection on day zero to contagiousness on day one, illness on day two and death by day six. They argue that such rapid incapacitation sits awkwardly with the film’s account of extensive transmission and an R0 rising to four.

They also note a mismatch between the stated mortality rate of around 20% and the emphasis on fatal cases: the film does not clearly show people becoming ill and recovering. Matt Damon’s character complicates the picture because he remains well despite exposure. The hosts discuss whether his different-coloured wristband acknowledges immunity, without resolving its basis. Nipah virus provides a comparison for the fictional disease’s fever and encephalitis, while Hendra virus enters the discussion as another bat-associated example.

### Vaccine allocation and the worldwide response

The vaccine rollout prompts a separate criticism. Allocation follows a birthday-based lottery rather than prioritising people described as especially susceptible, such as elderly or immunocompromised people. One host compares the mechanism with the US draft lottery.

Lee also raises the film’s US-centred response. The hosts would expect substantial involvement from China, the UK and other international partners. Although WHO appears, they describe its role as concentrated in a single character whose kidnapping largely removes that strand of the response.

Despite these objections, the hosts enjoy the film and appreciate its scientific care. Their overall verdict is that Hollywood compresses large teams, complicated decisions and long processes into a few characters and dramatic moments. They suggest Outbreak and The Andromeda Strain as possible future films to review.

## Show notes

In a two-part discussion, the hosts analyze the movie Contagion from their expert perspectives,
focusing on the film's portrayal of epidemiology and genomics. They note that the movie
compresses timelines for dramatic effect, speeding up the virus's spread and the response to
it, and that decisions about managing a crisis are based on societal values, not just science.

In part one, the hosts discuss the movie's depiction of the R0 value. They note that while this
explanation is useful for the audience, it is unlikely that an epidemiologist would need to
explain this concept to other epidemiologists. The group notes that the MEV1 virus is modeled
on the real-life Nipah virus and comment on a scene where the genome of the virus is described
as being 15 to 19 kilobases in length with 6 to 10 genes. They also discuss the movie's
depiction of virus isolation and the unrealistic speed with which the initial assessment of the
virus occurs. The podcasters touch upon the BSL4 lab and how the film depicts how the
scientists behave. They also discuss the character of Matt Damon, who is exposed to the virus
but does not get sick, and is an example of an asymptomatic carrier.

In part two, the bioinformaticians examine the "genome dashboard" scene, noting the software's
informative interface, which includes an alignment panel, protein structure, and a
recombination map. They also discuss a scene where a phylogenetic tree is used to determine a
change in the R0 value. They find this unrealistic because the tree is just a picture that
doesn't accurately represent how a virus spreads. The group discusses how the bioinformatician
is depicted in the film as moving in and out of the lab, which may have been realistic in 2011
but is less so in the present day. They discuss how the CDC is portrayed in the film, noting
scenes that were filmed at the actual CDC, as well as their experiences at the CDC. The
podcasters note the movie is very US-centric and that many international partners would be
involved in solving a global pandemic.
