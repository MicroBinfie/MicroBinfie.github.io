*SARS-CoV-2 genomics from research to public health*

For their first recording with all three hosts together in person, Lee Katz, Andrew Page and Nabil-Fareed Alikhan welcome Torsten Seemann. They compare their pandemic work, from investigating transmission and reinfection to building sequencing services and communicating results to public health teams. The discussion shows why useful genomic surveillance needs reliable laboratory processes, epidemiological context and established relationships—not just sequence data.

## In this episode

### Flights, fieldwork and laboratory lessons

Torsten describes how Australia's returning travellers provided introductions of SARS-CoV-2 despite closed borders. Travel histories and hotel-quarantine records gave investigators detailed epidemiological information. In some analyses, genomic evidence supported transmission during flights: passengers arriving from different countries had boarded the same aircraft at a hub such as Singapore. He thinks this led to some policy on masks, passenger separation and air filtration.

The panel wonders whether repeated rapid antigen tests or rapid PCR could track infection during a long flight. That leads to a discussion of portable laboratories, MinION fieldwork and improvised centrifuges. Torsten recalls a seven-day molecular biology course involving extraction, cloning, expression and capillary sequencing, plus several mishaps that earned him a destruction award. Lee's experience learning PFGE at CDC similarly helped him understand laboratory colleagues' work. Both accounts emphasise appreciating practical processes and where errors can enter.

### When genomic results trigger restrictions

Moving from research into public health meant adjusting to stricter controls and procedures. Torsten explains that this initially felt frustrating, but the consequences justified the caution. His team's genomic results helped uncover a major hotel-quarantine outbreak and contributed to a residential tower being locked down, affecting people confined to their building for weeks. Contamination or a sample swap could therefore have serious consequences.

Trust had been built before COVID through work on foodborne pathogens and consultations with epidemiologists and health officials. During the pandemic, bioinformaticians attended important meetings alongside genomic epidemiologists and medical microbiologists. Their role included explaining technical limitations: missing SARS-CoV-2 data, for example, could change a sample's position in a phylogenetic tree. Torsten describes bioinformaticians becoming visible participants in decision-making rather than remaining behind the scenes.

### Reinfection and the limits of consensus genomes

Lee recalls being asked early in the response whether people could be reinfected. He considered an all-versus-all genomic comparison of samples from successive infections and found an unnamed preprint involving Torsten that used the approach he had in mind. Torsten discusses comparisons of within-host and between-patient diversity, while acknowledging questionable data and limited time.

Consensus sequences alone could miss a mixture or a minor allele in an initial sample that later became dominant. Torsten says revisiting raw FASTQ data would have helped investigate that possibility. Publicly deposited reads offer opportunities for later studies, but only if metadata can connect samples from the same patient. The discussion also covers the SIREN project sampling healthcare workers and thresholds such as 90 days between positive tests, intended to help distinguish reinfection from a continuing infection.

### Turning research laboratories into COG-UK production

Andrew describes existing bacterial-genomics networks being rapidly drawn into COG-UK, with Nick Loman and Sharon Peacock among those mobilising collaborators. Local hospital connections and a shared campus with a regional viral diagnostic unit helped his team obtain samples. Sequencing expanded into bioinformatics analysis, national surveillance and international studies, while a research laboratory adopted the regular output and quality controls expected in public health.

Josh Quick's ARTIC protocol helped the team get started. Andrew recalls reaching production sequencing within about eight days of receiving the go-ahead, with ethics and contracts expedited. Sample collection began on 8 April 2020, and sequencing continued every week, including Christmas, until mid-April 2022. The panel also credits protocols.io as a useful resource for sharing protocols across countries.

Clinical identifiers were pseudonymised, not fully anonymous. Andrew describes NHS IT auditing CLIMB, two-factor authentication, restricted access and contractual commitments not to identify patients. Clinical laboratories retained the ability to link identifiers back to their records.

### Reporting outbreaks, not just SNP counts

The reporting problem changed during the pandemic. Early conversations concerned lineages, genetic distances and possible transmission links; later, attention shifted towards variants of concern such as Alpha and Beta. Reporting that samples differed by two or three SNPs did not directly answer a clinician's question about whether one patient infected another.

Andrew initially provided regional overviews of clusters and hotspots. A care-home investigation became a more concrete example: a lineage with two distinctive SNPs, combined with postcode mapping and patients' ages, linked six care homes. The discussion identifies staff moving between sites as an explanation for spread despite restrictions on visitors.

It took weeks to engage the relevant public health body, by which time the outbreaks had finished. However, the investigation established contacts for faster responses later. The panel also considers the practical constraints on prevention, including staffing, sick pay and the subsequent prioritisation of care-home testing and sequencing.

### Coordination across borders and vocabularies

Lee describes CDC's role as providing information, technical support and coordination, while state health laboratories held authority for much of the response. Laboratories initially sent samples to CDC, then increasingly sequenced locally and deposited sequences in GISAID or NCBI. Torsten contrasts this with Australia's working groups and committees, noting that Australia had no official national CDC at the time of recording.

The UK discussion distinguishes COG-UK's coordinating work from the responsibilities of the four nations' health agencies. A host observes that pathogens cross jurisdictions more readily than data: sharing agreements, material transfer agreements and other administrative arrangements were not always ready. As health agencies developed sequencing capacity, work moved back from academic partners.

The episode ends with another kind of translation problem: British geographical terminology, place-name pronunciation and regional differences in saying words such as data, phage and router.
