*SARS-CoV-2 variants and genomic surveillance in Africa*

Recorded on 19 February 2021, this episode brings Peter van Heusden together with hosts Lee Katz, Andrew Page and Nabil-Fareed Alikhan for an update on SARS-CoV-2 genomics. They discuss emerging variants, interpreting weak sequencing evidence, and the difficulty of sustaining genomic surveillance across Africa. The conversation distinguishes signals worth investigating from evidence strong enough to support public health conclusions.

## In this episode

### Emerging variants and recurring mutations

The roundup begins with B.1.1.7 carrying the spike mutation E484K, nicknamed Bleak. One host describes a linked outbreak in Bristol alongside independent appearances elsewhere in the UK. E484K is also discussed in relation to B.1.351, P.1 and P.2, with concern about its implications for vaccine performance.

The discussion broadens to B.1.525, associated with Nigeria or travel there, A.23.1 in Uganda, and C.2 in Zimbabwe. Not all these lineages carry E484K. The panel asks when an expanding lineage should count as concerning: a superspreading event can produce rapid growth without demonstrating an intrinsic advantage for the virus.

### Interpreting the South African vaccine trial

Peter describes the uncertainty surrounding the South African phase II AstraZeneca vaccine trial, in which he is a participant. He does not know whether he received vaccine or placebo. The trial involved about 2,000 people, with roughly 1,000 across both arms in the subgroup analysis following the emergence of B.1.351.

The reported efficacy estimate had very wide confidence limits, extending from around −40% to more than 50%. Peter stresses the small sample and the lack of statistical power to assess protection against severe disease. Participants were generally healthy, with an average age in their thirties. Against this uncertain evidence, he reports plans to donate South Africa’s AstraZeneca doses to the African Union.

### Making cautious use of low-coverage data

One host describes searching marginal Illumina data for E484K, sometimes down to a single supporting read. Several lineage-defining mutations appearing together can provide a useful signal even when a sample fails normal quality criteria. Such findings are passed to public health as high risk, low confidence, rather than treated as ordinary database submissions.

Public Health England’s published thresholds distinguish different levels of evidence for identifying variants. The discussion considers situations where 30–40% of samples fail GISAID criteria but may still contain useful information. Initial inspection with mpileup developed into spreadsheets and reports that make interesting mutations easier to spot.

Peter distinguishes one-read coverage from a single read contradicted by other reads, and says 5× coverage would give him more confidence. Wastewater is suggested as a possible early-warning application, although degraded RNA and poorly performing amplicons complicate that work.

### Names, bird lineages and recombination claims

The American birds preprint introduces nicknames for sub-lineages with spike changes at position 677. Peter connects this discussion to B.1.525, which also carries Q677H. The panel contrasts memorable names with Pango lineages, detailed UK lineage numbering and clade labels such as 20G and 20B. Geographic names become especially awkward when several distinct variants emerge in the same country.

Claims of recombination receive particular scrutiny. One host wants underlying reads, including linked mutations on a single high-quality read where possible, rather than an unusual consensus sequence alone. Cross-contamination, mixed infections and sequencing-run artefacts are alternative explanations that need checking before a striking sequence becomes a public announcement.

### Sustaining surveillance across Africa

Peter credits continuing sequencing efforts at KEMRI in Kenya, in Ghana, and at UVRI in Uganda. Their value lies not only in producing initial genomes but in returning with further samples over time. Elsewhere, promising work has stalled because reagents ran out, shipping was difficult or research funds used for emergency surveillance were exhausted. Moving from an academic initiative to routine public health provision remains a central challenge.

B.1.351 is reported moving northwards into Botswana and Zambia; the resources include a Zambia sequence report in MMWR. Sequencing with Zimbabwe for December and January found it in about 60% of samples in December, rising to about 90% at the time of recording. A.23.1 was also observed, alongside a lineage under investigation carrying a 501T mutation. Peter contrasts this with Uganda, where A.23.1 predominated, and reports that neither B.1.351 nor B.1.1.7 had taken over in the Ugandan and Kenyan data being discussed.

### Travel connections and surveillance gaps

Reports from Ghana describe B.1.1.7 in both community cases and travellers. Peter argues that variant distributions reflect human connections: flights link countries with the UK, Europe and Dubai, while spread from South Africa into neighbouring countries may follow road travel. Broader sequencing helps connect apparently isolated detections across countries.

Nabil-Fareed Alikhan reports that only 4.4% of the GISAID records he tabulated came from low- and middle-income countries. India ranked first and South Africa second among those countries by absolute sequence count, not population-adjusted contribution. The episode closes by noting both the large geographic blind spots and a Western and anglophone bias in how these conversations take place.
