*Contagion, part 1: R0, viral genomes and biosafety*

Lee Katz, Andrew Page and Nabil-Fareed Alikhan revisit the 2011 film Contagion, examining its pandemic science through their microbial bioinformatics and public health perspectives. In this first part, they discuss epidemiological briefings, the fictional MEV1 virus, early genome characterisation and laboratory containment. Their central distinction is between science that helps explain a crisis and the practical, ethical and political decisions needed to manage it.

## In this episode

### Watching Contagion after COVID-19

Following their earlier discussion of Jurassic Park, the hosts turn to Contagion. Its fictional MEV1 pandemic connects viral spread, containment, vaccine development and the social consequences of an outbreak. They broadly praise its combination of shoe-leather epidemiology, genomics and behavioural responses, while joking about its competent government response and missing toilet-paper panic.

The film predates COVID-19, but one host resists simply calling it prophetic. They recognise familiar problems, including growing a newly identified virus and organising a response under uncertainty. Their recurring criticism is compression: disease progression, scientific work and deliberation happen much faster than they would in practice.

### R0 explains spread, not what society should do

Kate Winslet's briefing scene introduces R0 through comparisons with seasonal flu, smallpox and polio. The film gives figures of about one, over three, and between four and six respectively. Lee questions why an epidemiologist would explain such a basic concept to other epidemiologists. Another host offers a different reading: the questioning official may be a lay participant, although her role is not established.

The discussion then separates scientific assessment from policy. Evidence of circulation does not itself determine whether to restrict activity; societal values and priorities matter too. The hosts discuss false alarms and emerging threats, including Zika, Ebola and H5N1 in raw milk, as examples of the uncertainty behind decisions that the film condenses into a short meeting.

### MEV1, Nipah and the missing experiment

The hosts identify Nipah virus as the model for MEV1, crediting scientific consultant Ian Lipkin. They connect the film's bat-to-pig origin with the account of Nipah's emergence discussed in the episode, while stressing that the fictional virus spreads more rapidly. MEV1 is explained as meningoencephalitis virus 1.

A professor describes the virus as 15–19 kilobases long, with 6–10 genes, typical of a paramyxovirus. One host points out that this describes the genome, not the virus itself. Lee questions the gene-count range and asks what experiment could already support those estimates.

The group considers whether this is only a rough initial assessment. They discuss Sanger and next-generation sequencing in the 2011 setting, contrasting this with metagenomic sequencing during COVID-19. Lee raises a gel or PFGE as possibilities, but nobody identifies a definite experimental basis for the character's claim.

### What high containment changes

The BSL-4 scenes prompt a discussion of protective suits, restricted access and research capacity. The hosts acknowledge that they have not worked in BSL-4 laboratories, so their assessment of the on-screen procedures is limited. They mention Ebola, Lassa, Nipah, Hendra and smallpox while discussing high-containment pathogens.

Their main concern is the practical effect of classification: requiring a scarce, specialised laboratory can sharply limit who can investigate a pathogen. They also raise difficulties moving samples and sequencing equipment across containment boundaries. Drawing comparisons with COVID-19 work, they discuss BSL-3 for cultured isolates and arrangements described as two-plus for diagnostics, with cultured material presenting a different exposure risk.

### Rule-breaking scientists and emergency capacity

The professor ignores an instruction to destroy his samples and subsequently finds a viable cell line. The hosts question the absence of consequences, suggesting that a real breach could threaten grants, affect the institution and bring legal repercussions. A scientist testing a vaccine on herself raises a similar concern. They recognise that the characters combine multiple roles, but worry that repeated misconduct undermines the portrayal of scientists and officials.

The emergency treatment centre raises another practical question: who pays? One host recalls a London conference centre fitted with 500 beds during COVID-19, including ventilators obtained from film sets and staffing drawn from retired doctors, junior doctors, vets and physiotherapists.

### Matt Damon's character: immune or asymptomatic?

Matt Damon's character is exposed but does not become ill. One host initially treats this as an opportunity to discuss asymptomatic infection. Another points out that the film does not establish whether he is completely immune for some unexplained reason, or was infected without symptoms and now has protective antibodies. It also leaves possible shedding unaddressed.

The hosts find his behaviour inconsistent: he is intensely protective of his daughter but moves around without a mask or comparable precautions. They interpret him as an everyman character who lets the audience see the unfolding crisis, while questioning what that storytelling choice communicates about infection and immunity.

## Show notes

In a two-part discussion, the hosts analyze the movie Contagion from their expert perspectives,
focusing on the film's portrayal of epidemiology and genomics. They note that the movie
compresses timelines for dramatic effect, speeding up the virus's spread and the response to
it, and that decisions about managing a crisis are based on societal values, not just science.

In part one, the hosts discuss the movie's depiction of the R0 value. They note that while this
explanation is useful for the audience, it is unlikely that an epidemiologist would need to
explain this concept to other epidemiologists. The group notes that the MEV1 virus is modeled
on the real-life Nipah virus and comment on a scene where the genome of the virus is described
as being 15 to 19 kilobases in length with 6 to 10 genes. They also discuss the movie's
depiction of virus isolation and the unrealistic speed with which the initial assessment of the
virus occurs. The podcasters touch upon the BSL4 lab and how the film depicts how the
scientists behave. They also discuss the character of Matt Damon, who is exposed to the virus
but does not get sick, and is an example of an asymptomatic carrier.

In part two, the bioinformaticians examine the "genome dashboard" scene, noting the software's
informative interface, which includes an alignment panel, protein structure, and a
recombination map. They also discuss a scene where a phylogenetic tree is used to determine a
change in the R0 value. They find this unrealistic because the tree is just a picture that
doesn't accurately represent how a virus spreads. The group discusses how the bioinformatician
is depicted in the film as moving in and out of the lab, which may have been realistic in 2011
but is less so in the present day. They discuss how the CDC is portrayed in the film, noting
scenes that were filmed at the actual CDC, as well as their experiences at the CDC. The
podcasters note the movie is very US-centric and that many international partners would be
involved in solving a global pandemic.
