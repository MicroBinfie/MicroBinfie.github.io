*SARS-CoV-2 sequencing in Denmark with Mads Albertsen*

Mads Albertsen joins the hosts to explain how his research group became part of Denmark's national SARS-CoV-2 sequencing effort. Recorded on 9 March 2021, the discussion covers sample logistics, changes to the ARTIC protocol, sequencing capacity and the legal barriers to using linked health data. It shows why successful surveillance depends on much more than access to sequencing instruments.

## In this episode

### From university closure to national surveillance

When Denmark closed university laboratories in March 2020, Albertsen asked a funding body whether he could redirect an existing grant and staff towards SARS-CoV-2. He contacted hospitals and the national laboratory, offering to sequence whatever samples they sent. His university reopened the laboratory for COVID work, and the group generated its first genomes within days using the ARTIC protocol and Nanopore sequencing.

Legal arrangements took longer than laboratory setup. Anonymised test samples could be sequenced within a week, but another two weeks were needed before the group could provide data linked to patient information. The shift also paused a project intended to sequence 10,000 samples across Denmark to investigate microbial diversity.

### Why sample logistics determine turnaround

The group receives plates of extracted RNA rather than handling the original diagnostic samples. Albertsen describes plates containing 88 samples, four negative controls, one positive control and two blank positions for concentration measurements. Standardised plates can pass through the workflow without rearrangement.

Denmark's community testing track uses large automated laboratories near the national laboratory. Positive samples are re-extracted and prepared for sequencing. Hospital samples require more manual handling and transport; collecting the original samples and re-extracting them proved easier than retrieving diagnostic RNA. Reported turnaround from sampling to sequence was about five days for community testing and ten days for hospital samples in national surveillance. Local hospital sequencing could support same-day turnaround for outbreaks.

### Running Nanopore at surveillance scale

Two plates are pooled and divided across two or three MinIONs, reducing the risk that one poor flow cell prevents sufficient data generation. Sequencing starts in the afternoon and stops after 10 gigabases per flow cell or at 6 am the next morning. Predictable delivery matters more than exhausting each flow cell's capacity. Washed flow cells are retained for development work and emergency use.

At the time of recording, the group was processing roughly 1,000 samples a day, with a potential capacity of 2,500. Each batch takes two laboratory days: cDNA and PCR work first, then pooling, normalisation and Nanopore preparation. During January's urgent response, the team reached 10,000 samples a week.

### Changing ARTIC barcoding and amplicon length

The original workflow's 24 available barcodes would have required too much laboratory labour at the intended scale. The group instead introduced barcodes during PCR, making around 500 barcodes from ten random nucleotides. It also increased amplicon length to approximately 1,200 bases, using roughly one-third of the ARTIC primers to make better use of Nanopore capacity.

A comparison of around 100 samples gave similar results to the original ARTIC protocol, and missing high-Ct samples did not turn out to be a big problem. Longer amplicons mean a dropout removes more of the genome, but fewer primer sites are exposed to mutations. The group rebalanced poorly performing primers. Albertsen says the method was shared on protocols.io, while fuller write-ups remained unfinished.

### Turning sequences into public-health decisions

National surveillance was particularly focused on B.1.1.7 and other variants of concern. Earlier mink-associated variants had prompted increased sequencing in November. Data generated around Christmas and New Year subsequently contributed to decisions to impose stricter lockdowns in Denmark.

One of the hosts explains how hospital outbreak analysis needs more than a lineage label: specialists must interpret clusters, distinguish separate outbreaks and work with healthcare staff. Albertsen identifies a shortage of people able to connect genomic analysis with clinical practice. Looking beyond COVID-19, the conversation considers sequencing for flu and bacterial outbreaks, including metagenomics. He sees sequencing cost as less of an obstacle than developing robust procedures that hospitals can integrate into routine work.

### Sharing data and handing over the programme

Denmark's national laboratory links samples to individuals and health records, while Albertsen's group connects genome data with healthcare data on its servers. The discussion covers uploads to GISAID and ENA. Around 50,000 sequences had been uploaded after approximately 100,000 samples were sequenced; repeat samples and records that could not be properly linked were among the exclusions.

Legal restrictions remained unresolved, including access to community-testing data for hospital investigations. Analyses could require collaboration with the national laboratory, creating a staffing bottleneck. Albertsen hoped to hand sequencing over to hospitals and the national laboratory by summer. His advice is to acknowledge everyone along the sample-to-action chain and build a team prepared to solve practical problems continually.
